How often residents ride the bus

Question 1 · all individual responses

The short version
  • Roughly 3 in 4 respondents are non-riders. The dominant, well-supported barriers are car dependence, service frequency and reliability, safety, and getting to the stop on foot.
  • Travel time is the loudest single complaint. The most-upvoted comment in the entire dataset (99 upvotes) is that the bus takes four times longer than driving.
  • Getting to the stop matters as much as the ride. Sidewalks and safe crossings to reach a stop are the number-one rated improvement and the most repeated top-upvoted theme.
  • Payment is a real but secondary friction. It ranks near the bottom of rated priorities, yet "tap-to-pay instead of the Umo app" is the single most-upvoted concrete ask on the fare question.

Do non-riders know how to pay?

Question 3 · non-riders vs. current riders

What this tells us
  • No single cause dominates the structured reasons. Schedule/frequency and safety lead.
  • People rate access-to-stop and reliability above everything. Sidewalks and safe crossings to reach a stop top the list; fare payment sits near the bottom.
  • Fare knowledge is genuinely thin. Only about a fifth of non-riders know how to pay, versus the large majority of current riders — an outreach gap more than a technology gap.
  • The fare reintroduction lands hardest on lower-income residents (see the income breakdown on the Key Evidence tab).

Fare knowledge, by gender

Question 3 · how residents answer "do you know how to pay?"

What stands out for women
  • Lighting at stops is the standout gap. Women rate it about half a point higher than men on a five-point scale — a large difference — alongside benches, sidewalks/crossings, and park-and-ride.
  • A fare-knowledge gap, not a fare-preference gap. Women are roughly twice as likely as men to say they would like to learn how to pay, even though both genders rate the payment system's importance almost identically.
  • Safety is more present in women's open-text answers (about 32% vs. 23% of men), though the structured "why I stopped" reason is noisier and doesn't show the same clean gap.
  • Overall ridership rates are similar between women and men; the difference is in what would make the experience feel safe and usable.

A fare-knowledge confidence gap

Question 3 · self-reported knowledge vs. willingness to ask

Women and men are equally likely to not actually know the fare system and default to assuming it works like other cities' (the same rate for both). Where they differ is how they describe that uncertainty: men are more likely to flatly claim they already know how to pay, while women are nearly twice as likely to say outright that they'd like to learn.

Key evidence & insights

The strongest evidence from both datasets, mapped directly against the draft problem statement. Each claim is marked by how well the survey supports it.

Draft problem statement

Despite encouraging signs of mode shift, transit ridership remains low. Over 70% of respondents indicated they had not used GoRaleigh services in the past 12 months. Residents frequently cite two key barriers: the additional time required to complete trips via transit, and the lack of convenient access to bus stops. Addressing these barriers is essential to expanding equitable access to transit, reducing car dependency, and achieving broader transportation and climate goals.
